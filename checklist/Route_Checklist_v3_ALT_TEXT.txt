Page 1 of a two-page checklist, titled "Can people with disabilities actually get there? A simple way to check the trip to a hospital or research site, by transit, car or ride." How it works: 1) walk the route from the transit stop or drop-off to the front desk, recording video and talking through what you see; 2) list every barrier: where it is, who it affects, and a suggested fix; 3) share the list with the site, the city and the transit agency, and check later whether it was fixed. Four photos from the Boston area: a subway entrance with no elevator and no sign to the one across the square; a temporary walkway between concrete barriers and traffic near a hospital; a peeled, broken warning pad for blind pedestrians at a crossing; a 2-inch gap between sidewalk and street that can stop a wheelchair wheel. Route checklist: transit stop (elevator at this entrance, working, clean, lit and easy to see; how many elevators on the whole trip); signs (a sign to the step-free route where people choose their way; simple words and symbols); distance and rest (seats, seats wherever people wait, shade, restrooms along the way); surface and slope (brick, cracks, gaps, roots, ice, puddles, stairs, hills, bumpy stretches, railings); crossings (curb ramp lined up, warning pad, accessible signal, walk time long enough for a slow walker); drop-off and parking (van loading zone, accessible parking, drop-off at the door for anyone who asks, placard or not); entrance (automatic door, button within reach, heavy doors, sign to the accessible door); inside (distance to the desk, desk height, kiosk usable without sight, elevator, restroom, seating, a place to lie down); senses (night lighting, glare, noise, crowds, smoke, smells, a quiet place to wait); the day itself (construction detours step-free and signed, snow and ice on ramps, ride pickup time). Footer: independent project by Uri Kartoun, DBbun LLC, entered in the NIH challenge on including people with disabilities in clinical research; not NIH-funded; prepared with AI assistance (Claude); free to use and adapt with credit (CC BY 4.0). Page 2 of the checklist, titled "Plan the visit so people can get there", for study teams and research offices: ask each participant what they need, and don't require proof or a placard. Many barriers can't be seen on a walk, and only the study team can fix them; most items came from people with disabilities and chronic illnesses who commented on earlier versions. Directions: send step-free directions with the visit invitation, in plain language with a photo of each step. Timing: schedule visits to fit the train or bus timetable, count waiting time, offer the first or last slot to avoid crowds. Ride: the study books and pays for the ride directly, with no up-front cost or wait-time fees; allow for paratransit pickup windows. Cost: reimburse fares, parking and rides, say how much per visit, acknowledge lost income and recovery time, and state the unit. Companion: someone can come along, and the study covers their travel. Communication: check-in and contact by text or email, not only phone; interpreters including ASL booked ahead; staff ready for AAC users or people who need more time. Rest: a place to lie down before and after, seats wherever people wait, a restroom near the visit room. Fewer trips: remote, home or hybrid visits, in person only for procedures. Ask: ask every participant what would make the trip possible, and offer the same help whether or not their disability is visible. Version 3, November 1, 2026.
