One-page summary titled "Can people with disabilities actually get there? A simple way to check the route from public transit to a hospital or research site." How it works: 1) walk the route from the transit stop to the front desk, recording video and describing what you see; 2) list every barrier, where it is, who it affects and a suggested fix; 3) share the list with the site, the city and the transit agency, and check later whether it was fixed. Four photos from the Boston area: a subway entrance with no elevator and no sign to the one across the square; a temporary walkway between concrete barriers and traffic near a hospital; a peeled, broken warning pad for blind pedestrians at a crossing; a 2-inch gap between sidewalk and street that can stop a wheelchair wheel. Route checklist: transit stop (elevator at this entrance and working, sign to the accessible entrance); distance and rest (seats, shade); surface (brick, cracks, gaps, roots, ice, puddles); crossings (curb ramp lined up, warning pad, accessible signal, enough time); drop-off (van loading zone, accessible parking); entrance (automatic door, reachable button, heavy doors, signs); inside (distance to desk, desk height, elevator, signs, restroom, seating); senses (night lighting, glare, noise, smoke, smells); the day itself (construction detours, weather, ride pickup time). Footer: independent project by Uri Kartoun, DBbun LLC, entered in the NIH challenge on including people with disabilities in clinical research; not NIH-funded; prepared with AI assistance (Claude).
